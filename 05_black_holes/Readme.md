05 - Black Holes
Introduction

Black holes are among the most fascinating and mysterious objects in the universe. They represent regions of spacetime where gravity becomes so strong that nothing—not even light—can escape once it crosses a certain boundary.

Although they appear like "holes" in space, black holes are actually extremely compact objects formed when massive stars collapse under their own gravity.

Black holes are predicted by Albert Einstein's General Theory of Relativity and have been confirmed through astronomical observations.
 
What is a black Holes?s

A black hole is a region of spacetime where gravity is so powerful that the escape velocity exceeds the speed of light.

Since nothing can travel faster than light, anything crossing the boundary of a black hole becomes trapped.

This includes:

Matter
Light
Radiation
Planets
Stars
How Do Black Holes Form?

Most black holes form from the death of very massive stars.

The process occurs in several stages:

A massive star burns nuclear fuel.
Fusion stops.
Gravity overcomes internal pressure.
The star collapses rapidly.
If enough mass remains, a black hole forms.

Smaller stars become white dwarfs.

More massive stars become neutron stars.

The most massive stars become black holes.

Structure of a Black Hole
Event Horizon

The event horizon is the "point of no return."

Once an object crosses this boundary, escape is impossible.

The radius of this boundary is called the Schwarzschild Radius.

Singularity

According to General Relativity, all the mass of the black hole is concentrated into an infinitely small point called the singularity.

Modern physics suggests that a future theory of Quantum Gravity may provide a more complete description of this region.

Schwarzschild Radius

The Schwarzschild Radius defines the size of a non-rotating black hole.

Any object compressed inside this radius becomes a black hole.

Examples:

Earth:

≈ 9 mm

Sun:

≈ 2.95 km

This demonstrates that black holes are incredibly dense rather than simply massive.

Escape Velocity

Escape velocity is the minimum speed required to escape an object's gravity.

Earth:

11.2 km/s

Moon:

2.38 km/s

Black Hole:

Greater than the speed of light.

Since nothing can travel faster than light, escape becomes impossible.

Time Dilation

Einstein showed that gravity affects time itself.

Near a black hole:

Time moves more slowly.
Light becomes redshifted.
Clocks tick slower.

This phenomenon is called gravitational time dilation.

A person far away would observe someone near a black hole aging more slowly.

Hawking Radiation

In 1974, Stephen Hawking proposed that black holes are not completely black.

Quantum effects near the event horizon allow black holes to emit tiny amounts of radiation.

Over incredibly long periods, black holes slowly lose mass.

This process is called Hawking Radiation.

Types of Black Holes
Stellar Black Holes

Mass:

3–100 Solar Masses

Formed from collapsing massive stars.

Supermassive Black Holes

Millions to billions of solar masses.

Found at the centers of galaxies.

Example:

Sagittarius A*

Intermediate Black Holes

Mass between stellar and supermassive black holes.

Their formation is still an active area of research.

Primordial Black Holes (Hypothetical)

These may have formed shortly after the Big Bang.

Their existence has not yet been confirmed.

Famous Black Holes

Key Takeaways
Black holes are predicted by General Relativity.
Gravity near black holes is so strong that light cannot escape.
The event horizon marks the point of no return.
Time flows more slowly near black holes.
Black holes can slowly evaporate through Hawking Radiation.
Black holes are among the most extreme environments in the universe.

Black holes continue to be one of the greatest laboratories for testing the laws of physics, helping scientists explore gravity, quantum mechanics, and the evolution of the universe.
